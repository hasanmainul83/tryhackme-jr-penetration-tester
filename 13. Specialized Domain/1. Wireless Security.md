# Wireless Security — Practical Notes

## 1. Wireless Frequency Spectrum

The **wireless frequency spectrum** is the range of radio frequencies used to transmit data wirelessly. Different technologies operate at different frequencies and serve different purposes.

|Technology|Frequency|Common Uses|
|---|---|---|
|RFID|125 kHz–960 MHz (varies by type)|Asset tracking, access cards|
|NFC|13.56 MHz|Contactless payments, card interactions|
|Bluetooth|2.4 GHz|Device pairing, headphones, peripherals|
|Wi-Fi|2.4 GHz and 5 GHz|Wireless LAN and Internet access|

**Remember:** Bluetooth and Wi-Fi both use the 2.4 GHz band, so interference can occur.

## 2. Typical Wi-Fi Network Topology

```
             INTERNET
                 |
             ROUTER / AP
              192.168.1.1
           /       |       \
       Laptop     Phone     IoT
       .1.10      .1.11     .1.20

    Attacker: Monitor Mode
    Passively captures wireless frames
```

- **Router:** Routes traffic between networks.
    
- **Access Point (AP):** Allows wireless devices to connect to a network; commonly integrated into home routers.
    
- **Client:** A laptop, phone, or IoT device connected to Wi-Fi.
    
- **Monitor mode:** Allows a compatible wireless adapter to capture wireless frames without associating with the AP as a normal client.
    

**Security concept:** Wireless signals travel through the air, so nearby devices may observe transmitted frames. Whether useful data can be read depends on encryption and the type of traffic captured.

## 3. Wi-Fi Security

Wi-Fi follows the **IEEE 802.11** standards.

- **Wi-Fi 5:** Common modern Wi-Fi generation.
    
- **Wi-Fi 6:** Newer generation commonly deployed with modern devices.
    

### Authentication & Encryption

|Protocol|Security|
|---|---|
|WEP|Deprecated; weak encryption|
|WPA|Older replacement for WEP; uses TKIP|
|WPA2|Uses AES-based CCMP in its standard secure configuration|
|WPA3|Uses SAE for password-based authentication and improves resistance to offline dictionary attacks|

### Common Wi-Fi Misconfigurations

- Weak Wi-Fi passphrases.
    
- Outdated WEP or WPA configurations.
    
- WPS enabled unnecessarily.
    
- Default router administrator credentials.
    
- Outdated AP firmware.
    
- Poor segmentation between guest and internal networks.
    

### Common Wi-Fi Attacks

**1. Password attacks**

- Attempt to recover weak Wi-Fi passwords.
    
- Success depends on the security protocol, authentication method, and password strength.
    

**2. Rogue Access Point**

- An unauthorized AP connected to the organization's network.
    
- May introduce an unmonitored entry point into the internal network.
    

**3. Evil Twin**

- A fake AP mimics a legitimate network's SSID.
    
- Tricks users into connecting to the attacker's AP.
    
- May be used to intercept traffic or collect credentials.
    

**4. Deauthentication**

- Sends frames intended to disconnect wireless clients.
    
- Can disrupt connectivity and, in some situations, encourage clients to reconnect.
    

**5. Traffic interception**

- Attempts to capture wireless communications.
    
- Weak encryption or insecure application protocols can expose sensitive information.
    

### Wi-Fi Defenses

- Use WPA2 or WPA3 with strong passphrases.
    
- Disable WEP and unnecessary WPS.
    
- Change default router administrator credentials.
    
- Update AP firmware.
    
- Separate guest and IoT devices from sensitive systems.
    
- Monitor for unauthorized APs and abnormal wireless activity.

## 4. Bluetooth Security

Bluetooth operates in the **2.4 GHz** band and is used for short-range communication between devices.

### Classic Bluetooth vs BLE

|Feature|Classic Bluetooth|Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE)|
|---|---|---|
|Typical use|Audio streaming, file transfer|Sensors, fitness trackers, smart locks|
|Communication pattern|Often sustained connections|Small, intermittent data transfers|
|Security mechanisms|Secure Simple Pairing (SSP)|Several pairing methods, including LE Secure Connections|

### Pairing Methods

- **Legacy PIN pairing:** Older systems may use short PINs such as `0000` or `1234`.
    
- **Secure Simple Pairing (SSP):** Introduced with Bluetooth 2.1; uses cryptographic key exchange.
    
- **LE Legacy Pairing:** Used by older BLE implementations.
    
- **LE Secure Connections:** Introduced with Bluetooth 4.2; improves pairing security.
    
- **Just Works:** A BLE pairing method without meaningful user authentication to protect against an active man-in-the-middle attack. It does not provide MITM protection merely because encryption is established.
    

### Common Bluetooth Attacks

- **Unnecessary discoverability:** Makes devices easier to identify nearby.
    
- **Weak pairing:** Short or default PINs may be guessed.
    
- **Unauthorized pairing:** Weak approval controls can allow unwanted connections.
    
- **Bluejacking:** Sending unsolicited messages.
    
- **Bluesnarfing:** Unauthorized access to data over Bluetooth.
    
- **Bluebugging:** Exploiting vulnerabilities to control device functions.
    
- **Outdated firmware:** Leaves known vulnerabilities unpatched.
    

### Bluetooth Defenses

- Turn off discoverability when not needed.
    
- Remove unknown or unused paired devices.
    
- Use secure pairing with user verification whenever possible.
    
- Avoid legacy pairing and default PINs.
    
- Keep device firmware updated.
    
- Disable Bluetooth when it is unnecessary.

## 5. RFID vs NFC

**RFID (Radio-Frequency Identification):** Uses electromagnetic fields to identify or track tagged objects.

**NFC (Near-Field Communication):** A short-range technology operating at **13.56 MHz**, supporting bidirectional communication over approximately 5 cm.

|Feature|RFID|NFC|
|---|---|---|
|Main purpose|Identification and tracking|Contactless interaction and data exchange|
|Range|Varies by tag and frequency; can reach much farther than NFC|Typically approximately 5 cm|
|Examples|Inventory tags, access badges|Contactless payments, transit cards|
|Components|Reader and tag|Reader/writer and compatible device or tag|

### RFID Tag Types

- **Passive:** Draws energy from the reader's electromagnetic field; inexpensive and generally shorter range.
    
- **Active:** Has its own battery and can communicate over greater distances.
    

### Common RFID/NFC Attacks

- **Eavesdropping:** Capturing wireless communications.
    
- **Cloning:** Copying data from a tag or card when its design permits it.
    
- **Relay attack:** Forwarding communication between a legitimate card and a reader to extend the effective communication distance.
    
- **Skimming:** Unauthorized scanning of a card to obtain its data.
    
- **Lost or stolen card:** A physical credential may provide access until it is revoked.
    

### RFID/NFC Defenses

- Use cards with appropriate cryptographic authentication.
    
- Minimize sensitive information stored directly on cards.
    
- Revoke lost or stolen credentials immediately.
    
- Review access permissions regularly.
    
- Use protective sleeves where appropriate.
    
- Add a second authentication factor for sensitive access when supported.

## 6. Other Wireless Technologies

|Technology|Purpose|Main Security Risks|
|---|---|---|
|Zigbee|Low-power mesh networks for smart devices|Weak/default joining keys; compromised nodes may affect other devices|
|Z-Wave|Home automation|Downgrade risks when legacy S0 security is accepted|
|LoRa / LoRaWAN|Long-range, low-power IoT|Static keys, replay risks, frame-counter problems|
|LTE-M / NB-IoT|Cellular IoT connectivity|Physical tampering, outdated firmware, exposed management interfaces|
|Infrared|Short-range, line-of-sight control|Command capture and replay; typically lacks authentication or encryption|

### Zigbee

- Used by smart lights, sensors, and plugs.
    
- Uses mesh networking, where devices can relay traffic.
    
- **Risk:** A weak device-joining process may expose network keys.
    
- **Defense:** Use install codes and securely provision each device.
    

### Z-Wave

- Used by smart locks, thermostats, blinds, and lights.
    
- **S0:** Older security framework with known weaknesses in key exchange.
    
- **S2:** Improves security using cryptographic key exchange.
    
- **Risk:** Accepting weaker legacy pairing may enable downgrade attacks.
    
- **Defense:** Prefer S2 and disable unnecessary S0 compatibility.
    

### LoRa / LoRaWAN

- Used for agricultural sensors, environmental monitoring, and smart infrastructure.
    
- **ABP (Activation by Personalization):** Uses configured session keys that may remain static.
    
- **Risk:** Poor key management and frame-counter problems can enable replay attacks.
    
- **OTAA (Over-the-Air Activation):** Establishes fresh session keys during joins.
    
- **Defense:** Prefer OTAA where feasible; protect and rotate keys appropriately and monitor frame counters.
    

### Cellular IoT

- Examples: LTE-M and NB-IoT.
    
- **Risk:** Devices in remote locations may be physically accessible, difficult to update, or exposed through insecure management interfaces.
    
- **Defense:** Change default credentials, restrict management access, use private connectivity or VPNs where suitable, and update firmware.
    

### Infrared

- Uses light signals and typically requires line of sight.
    
- Common in TV and air-conditioner remotes.
    
- **Risk:** Commands may be captured and replayed because many implementations lack authentication or encryption.
    
- **Defense:** Disable unused infrared receivers when possible and physically protect devices that respond to infrared commands.

## 7. Key Wireless Security Terms

|Term|Meaning|
|---|---|
|SSID|Human-readable Wi-Fi network name|
|BSSID|Identifier, commonly the AP radio's MAC address, for a Wi-Fi access point/basic service set|
|WEP|Deprecated Wi-Fi security protocol with serious encryption weaknesses|
|WPA|Wi-Fi security protocol introduced as a replacement for WEP|
|WPA2|Wi-Fi security generation commonly using AES-based CCMP|
|WPA3|Newer Wi-Fi security generation using SAE for personal-mode authentication|
|SAE|Simultaneous Authentication of Equals; WPA3-Personal authentication mechanism|
|MITM|Man-in-the-Middle; intercepting and potentially altering communication|
|BLE|Bluetooth Low Energy; designed for power-efficient communication|
|SSP|Secure Simple Pairing; Bluetooth pairing mechanism introduced in Bluetooth 2.1|
|RFID|Radio-Frequency Identification|
|NFC|Near-Field Communication|
|Zigbee|Low-power wireless mesh networking for IoT|
|Z-Wave|Wireless home-automation protocol|
|LoRa|Long-range, low-power wireless technology for IoT|
|Infrared|Light-based, short-range communication|

## Core Assessment Workflow

**Identify the wireless technology → Check its frequency and intended use → Inspect authentication and encryption → Look for weak passwords, insecure pairing, default credentials, and outdated firmware → Assess exposure from rogue devices and traffic interception → Recommend segmentation, secure configuration, updates, and monitoring.**
